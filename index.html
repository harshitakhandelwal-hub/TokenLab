Tokenization

First the text is split at spaces and punctuation. Then each word is looked up. If the vocabulary knows it, it stays whole. If not, it is cut into smaller pieces the vocabulary does know.

A piece that starts with ## continues the word before it. [UNK] means there was no piece at all.

3

Vocabulary

The vocabulary is a fixed dictionary: every token the tokenizer knows, each with its own ID. Once built, it never changes, even when new words arrive.

Four IDs are reserved. [PAD] fills empty slots, [UNK] stands in for anything unknown, [BOS] and [EOS] mark the start and end of a sequence.

Token IDs

This list of numbers is all the model receives. Each ID picks one row of the model's embedding table, which is where learning about meaning begins.

Decoding runs the dictionary backwards. Lowercasing and [UNK] throw information away, so the text you get back may not match what you typed.

Frequency

The counts come from running the finished tokenizer over the training corpus. A handful of tokens do most of the work, and most tokens are rare.

That pattern is Zipf's law, and it is why tokenizers keep common words whole and build rare words from pieces.

Embeddings

Inside a model, each token ID picks out a list of numbers called its embedding. Tokens used in similar places get similar numbers, so they end up close together.

These are real embeddings, trained live on your corpus with word2vec (skip-gram). Each token gets 16 numbers, and PCA squashes them down to the 3 directions you see.

How this compares with real LLM tokenizers

GPT-style tokenizers use byte-level BPE: their base alphabet is all 256 bytes, so nothing is ever [UNK], and spaces are kept inside tokens instead of being thrown away. Their vocabularies hold 50,000 to 200,000 tokens learned from billions of words. The steps are the same as here: split the text, build pieces, look up IDs.
